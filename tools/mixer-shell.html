M8TRX

A performance layer over a finished song. It cuts the whole band into slices and rebuilds them as a recipe — a form of named parts, each part a row of slices — which you then play, edit and reroll. It never changes the song. Nothing here writes to the arrangement, the mix, the notes or the game's copy of the track, and nothing is kept unless you save it.

Reading the timeline

One row per part, drawn to a single time scale: a bar is the same width everywhere, so a four-bar part runs half as long as an eight-bar one and parts can be compared by eye. Each block is one slice, as wide as the time it takes, and coloured by the piece of the song it came from — the same colour anywhere means the same material returning. A hatched block is muted and still takes its time; a thick left edge is where a part starts saying itself again; a small square marks a fill.

Holding things

Click a slice to take it, ⌘/ctrl-click to add or remove one, or press and drag across a run — across rows too. Click a part's card to take that whole part. Double-click anything to play from it. Whatever you are holding is what the rails above the timeline act on, and each rail says in words what it would hit: “2 slices in B · Verse”, “all of B”, “nothing held”.

Solid acts now, dashed waits

A control's band is its scope. The rows stack in a fixed order, so the meaning of a row never changes under you: the top band builds, the one under it holds the whole recipe, and the rails below act on what you are holding. Everything in the top band is drawn dashed, which means it changes nothing until you press Go; a solid border acts the moment you press it. While an edit is waiting for the next bar the whole timeline draws dashed too.

Elsewhere on the desk

While a recipe is live the desk lends M8TRX three of its own controls, each relabelled so you can see it has: Undo M8TRX (M8TRX's history only — your mix and arrangement history is kept separately and comes back untouched), Recipe Once / Recipe Loop, and this ?. The M8TRX button in the toolbar is how you leave; Exit M8TRX clears the recipe altogether.

Layout & timing

Playback safety

Output stage. Larger buffers can absorb short output spikes, but add real key-to-sound latency and need a reload.

Scheduling stage. More read-ahead protects against busy-browser stalls; larger values make edits and loops take longer to be heard.

Synthesis stage. On, every MRDR-3 lane is rendered per sample on the audio thread — about three times cheaper on dense material, and it uses no note cache at all, so a cold cache cannot stall the transport. Off, those lanes go back to the older native engine, which builds nodes per note and leans on the cache. Either way the change is heard from the next note: anything already sounding finishes on the backend it started on. This is a bench switch, not a song setting — it never leaves this session and is never saved.

Caching & diagnostics

These tools help inspect playback on this device. They never become part of the song.

How they work together. The buffer protects delivery from the audio thread to the speakers; read-ahead protects the main-thread scheduler that feeds it. For a busy song, try Smooth playback with 500 ms or 1 second read-ahead before Maximum safety.

Loop diagnostics

While the transport plays, the Mixer records each heard loop plus immediate rows for playback interruptions, complete output loss, repair attempts, recovery, and freeze exports. Profile Track Load stops playback and measures every visible track over the song’s densest eight bars, both with and without that track’s inserts. Rows persist across reloads.
